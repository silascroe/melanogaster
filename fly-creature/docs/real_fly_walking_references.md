# Real fruit-fly walking: animation references

Research notes for the **authored visual gait** in `fly-creature`. These observations concern *Drosophila melanogaster* walking forward on a surface. They do not establish that our selected connectome circuit generates walking, or that the current Godot rig is a biomechanical simulation.

## What the papers show

- A walking fly usually has one diagonal group of feet supporting it while the other group steps: right front + left middle + right hind, then left front + right middle + left hind. The grouping is real, but perfect three-at-once stepping is an idealization. Chun et al. found a **modified tripod** across the measured speed range: front foot leads the middle foot, which leads the hind foot by small delays within each group; the opposite group is about half a cycle away. The delay and its variability decrease as speed rises. [Chun et al., eLife 2021](https://elifesciences.org/articles/65878) (Results, “Flies employ interleg coordination close to tripod across speeds).
- A broader sample found a *continuum* of coordination rather than separate, hard gait switches. Slower bouts can look tetrapod-like or noncanonical, but the most common coordination stays near tripod; left/right counterparts remain about half a cycle apart. Thus “slow walk = switch to a pure tetrapod” is too rigid for this project. [DeAngelis et al., eLife 2019](https://elifesciences.org/articles/46409) (Results, limb phase distributions); [Mendes et al., eLife 2013](https://elifesciences.org/articles/00231) (slow, medium, fast walking comparisons).
- Speed changes come mainly from **stance duration**: each tarsus remains on its foothold longer during a slow walk. Swing duration changes much less. Swing length changes less than stance duration too. Shorten the planted interval for faster walking before exaggerating leg lift. [Chun et al., eLife 2021](https://elifesciences.org/articles/65878) (Figure 2D and Results); [Mendes et al., eLife 2013](https://elifesciences.org/articles/00231) (Figure 2B–C); [DeAngelis et al., eLife 2019](https://elifesciences.org/articles/46409) (Figure 1E).
- In **stance**, the tarsus is approximately stationary against the ground while the thorax advances over it, so the leg appears to sweep backward relative to the body. It lifts at its rearward extreme, travels forward through the air, and lands at an anterior foothold. The front, middle, and hind legs have distinct trajectories and joint-angle profiles; six identical sinusoidal legs will look artificial. [Mendes et al., eLife 2013](https://elifesciences.org/articles/00231) (footprint/step definitions); [Natural variability and individuality of walking behavior in Drosophila, JEB 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11607691/) (AEP/PEP definitions); [3D joint-kinematics study, Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2024.1357598/full).
- The fly's body is not a frozen platform. Its center of mass rises slightly and moves fastest around the middle of tripod stance; faster flies stand somewhat higher. Keep that motion subtle, rather than adding a large mammal-like bounce. [Chun et al., eLife 2021](https://elifesciences.org/articles/65878) (Figures 2B and 4A–B).
- Forward walking means the body axis points broadly along travel. A sideways displacement with the body remaining crosswise is **crab walking**, a different behavior. During a modest turn, the outside legs take longer steps than inside legs; strong turns can also change step direction and timing. [DeAngelis et al., eLife 2019](https://elifesciences.org/articles/46409) (turning results); [Rayshubskiy et al., eLife 2025](https://elifesciences.org/articles/102230) (steering kinematics); [Strauss & Heisenberg, J Comp Physiol A 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2121965/) (straight walking and turning).

Real female fly at the top, biomechanical model below, slowed to **0.05×**. [Direct MOV download](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fncomms14494/MediaObjects/41467_2017_BFncomms14494_MOESM1950_ESM.mov). See the paper's Supplementary information if the section anchor moves. The paper states CC BY 4.0 for its content unless a separate credit says otherwise; attribute authors, article, and license if reusing frames. This is a reference clip, **not** an asset we have incorporated.
2. **Best top + side/footfall reference:** [Chun et al., eLife 2021, Video 1](https://elifesciences.org/articles/65878#video1). Shows a real fly from top and side with marked center of mass and planted feet. The study's Figures 2–4 pair it with measured timing and body motion. eLife article content is CC BY, with attribution required for reuse.
3. **Clear underside of normal walking:** [Chadha & Cook, PLOS ONE 2014, Supplementary Video 3 on Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The-Effect-of-Stress-on-Motor-Function-in-Drosophila-pone.0112076.s003.ogv). A normal fly seen from below, slowed **33×**, with alternating support triangles indicated. The file page lists **CC BY 4.0** and names Chadha and Cook. Useful for which feet touch, less useful for body silhouette.
4. **Another real-foot contact view:** [Mendes et al., eLife 2013, unprocessed Video 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3545443/#video1) and [processed Video 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3545443/#video2). The unprocessed optical video shows tarsal contacts; processed version tracks footprint and body. Article says **CC BY** with author/source attribution.
5. **Variable speed and turns, model comparison:** [Whole-body physics simulation of fruit fly locomotion, Nature 2025, Supplementary Video 2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09029-4#Sec35). It shows a simulated fly imitating measured walking, including a real trajectory, straight walking, and a turn. Use as a secondary animation reference: the rendered animal is a model, not raw fly footage.

The Nature direct video URL above was obtained from the paper's own download link; I have **not** embedded or redistributed any of these videos. Article/section links are preferable when sharing because they include captions and attribution.

## Practical target for our Godot rig

1. **Fix travel direction first.** For a forward-walking shot, keep the fly's head and thorax pointing down its path. Place the camera to show a three-quarter side view. A genuinely lateral step needs its own animation, not the forward gait rotated ninety degrees.
2. Preserve diagonal grouping, but offset lift-off/touchdown slightly in **front → middle → hind** order inside each group. Let the offsets shrink when moving faster. Allow small per-leg timing variation at slow speed; keep opposite-side matching legs roughly half a cycle apart. These are animation choices inspired by the studies, not fitted numerical parameters.
3. Hold stance feet in world space until lift-off, then use a **short, low** forward recovery arc and land them ahead of the body. Front, middle, and hind feet should have different home positions and modestly different paths. Foot travel should stay within joint reach. Reduce the conspicuous high-knee look.
4. For a slower, readable walk, lengthen stance and leave swing comparatively brief. Use moderate forward body speed; don't merely play a fast leg cycle in slow motion while translating at the old speed. A start, a few straight steps, and a stop would make the footage easier to compare with real clips.
5. Add a **small** up/down body motion and very slight pitch over the support cycle. On gentle turns, shorten inside strides and lengthen outside strides. Validate straight walking before adding turn complexity.

No exact Godot timings, arc heights, or phase offsets above are measured from our model. Those require matching its scale and rig to the footage and then visually comparing rendered movement at normal speed and slow playback. Record the chosen values as authored animation parameters, not measured biological constants.
